# The Hubble Constant and Hubble Tension

We know that the universe is expanding. Astronomer Edwin Hubble, for whom the Hubble Space Telescope is named, discovered cosmic expansion in 1929, observing that "the farther away a galaxy is from Earth, the faster it appears to be receding from us."

A fundamental question persists: what describes the relationship between distance and recession velocity? This value is called the Hubble Constant. Determining it provides answers to critical questions, including expansion speed and the universe's precise age.

The Hubble Space Telescope achieved unprecedented precision in measuring the Hubble Constant — nearly one percent accuracy. Before its launch, age estimates ranged from 9.7 to 19.5 billion years. Following Hubble observations, astronomers refined this to approximately 13.8 billion years.

Hubble accomplished this by observing distant objects like Cepheid variable stars and Type 1a supernovae with known brightnesses. By comparing their actual brightness to their apparent brightness, astronomers can calculate distances, similar to "judging the distance to approaching headlights by calculating how bright they appear in the dark."

## The Measurement Methods

Two primary approaches exist for measuring the Hubble Constant. Space telescopes observe Cepheid variables and Type 1a supernovae, yielding measurements of 70-76 kilometers per second per megaparsec. Alternatively, observations of the cosmic microwave background provide measurements of approximately 67-68 kilometers per second per megaparsec.

This discrepancy is termed the Hubble Tension. Scientists propose that exotic particles, alternative gravity theories, or early dark energy forms might explain the difference, but further research remains necessary.
