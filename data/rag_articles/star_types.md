# Star Types

The universe's stars range in brightness, size, color, and behavior. Some types change into others very quickly, while others stay relatively unchanged over trillions of years.

## Main Sequence Stars

A star forms when dust and gas clump together in a stellar nursery. Over centuries, the clump gains mass, begins rotating, and heats up. When temperatures in the core reach millions of degrees, nuclear fusion initiates. This process occurs when two protons, the nuclei of hydrogen atoms, merge to form one helium nucleus.

Stars undergoing hydrogen-to-helium fusion are classified as main sequence stars. Main sequence stars make up around 90% of the universe's stellar population. They exhibit considerable variation in luminosity, color, and size—ranging from one-tenth to 200 solar masses—and persist for millions to billions of years.

Observable main sequence stars include Sirius, the brightest star visible without optical aid, and Rigil Kentaurus (Alpha Centauri), the nearest main sequence star visible to the unaided eye.

## Red Giants

When main sequence stars with less than eight solar masses exhaust their hydrogen core supply, gravitational collapse begins. Increased pressure and temperature trigger helium fusion into carbon, releasing energy that expands the star's outer layers dramatically, creating a red giant.

Eventually, the red giant becomes unstable and begins pulsating, periodically expanding and ejecting some of its atmosphere. All outer layers eventually disperse, creating an expanding dust and gas cloud called a planetary nebula. Our Sun will experience this transformation approximately 5 billion years from now.

Arcturus and Gamma Crucis represent visible red giants observable without telescopes.

## White Dwarfs

After shedding atmospheric material, only the stellar core remains—a white dwarf. A white dwarf is usually Earth-size but hundreds of thousands of times more massive. A teaspoon of its material would weigh more than a pickup truck.

White dwarfs generate no new heat internally and gradually cool across billions of years. Despite their name, white dwarfs can emit visible light that ranges from blue white to red. Scientists frequently discover dusty disks surrounding white dwarfs, containing material remnants and even planets from the earlier red giant phase.

The Sun will eventually become a white dwarf approximately 10 billion years after its red giant phase concludes.

White dwarfs remain too dim for unaided observation, though some appear in binary systems with visible main sequence stars. With telescopes, observers can locate LP 145-141 and Van Maanen's star.

## Neutron Stars

Neutron stars are stellar remnants that pack more mass than the Sun into a sphere about as wide as New York City's Manhattan Island is long.

Neutron stars form when main sequence stars containing eight to twenty solar masses exhaust their hydrogen. The star progresses through successive fusion stages—helium to carbon, carbon to neon, neon to oxygen, oxygen to silicon, and finally silicon to iron. Each stage produces diminishing energy returns.

By the time silicon fuses into iron, the star runs out of fuel in a matter of days. Fusing iron requires energy rather than releasing it. The core collapses catastrophically then rebounds violently, creating a shockwave that tears through the star in a tremendous explosion called a supernova.

**Pulsars** are rapidly rotating neutron stars with bright X-ray hot spots on their surfaces that spin in and out of view like lighthouse beams. Some rotate faster than blender blades.

**Magnetars** possess magnetic fields 10 trillion times stronger than a refrigerator magnet's and up to a thousand times stronger than a typical neutron star's.

Neutron stars remain too faint for unaided observation, though the Hubble Space Telescope has captured some in visible light. Astronomers typically observe them through X-ray and radio emissions.

## Red Dwarfs

Red dwarfs represent the smallest main sequence stars—merely fractions of the Sun's size and mass. They rank among the coolest stars and appear more orange than red. When a red dwarf produces helium via fusion in its core, the released energy brings material to the star's surface, where it cools and sinks back down, taking along a fresh supply of hydrogen to the core.

This continuous circulation permits red dwarfs to steadily consume their entire hydrogen supply over trillions of years without structural changes, unlike other stellar types. Scientists estimate that low-mass red dwarfs containing approximately one-third solar mass may possess lifespans exceeding the current universe age—potentially reaching 14 trillion years.

Red dwarfs form in substantially greater numbers than massive stars. Combined with their exceptional longevity, red dwarfs make up around 75% of the Milky Way galaxy's stellar population.

Red dwarfs prove too faint for unaided viewing but may be visible with backyard telescopes, including Lacaille 8760 and Lalande 21185.

## Brown Dwarfs

Brown dwarfs aren't technically stars. They're more massive than planets but not quite as massive as stars. Generally containing between 13 and 80 Jupiter masses, brown dwarfs emit virtually no visible light, though scientists have observed some through infrared detection.

Some brown dwarfs form identically to main sequence stars from nebular gas and dust clumps but never accumulate sufficient mass for substantial stellar fusion. Others may form through planetary mechanisms within stellar disk systems.

Brown dwarfs remain invisible to both unaided eyes and backyard telescopes.
